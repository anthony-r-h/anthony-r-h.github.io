Heading: What Is a Dulles Bag?
Subheading: Introduction to the Dulles briefcase

In present-day Japan, the Dulles briefcase is generally regarded as a business briefcase distinctive to mature men. Some even crown it the "king of leather bags."

With its substantial leather body, a metal-frame opening that can be opened wide, an imposing tongue clasp and a shining lock, it has a regal presence that people find hard to put down. The highly attractive Dulles briefcase has come to be known as the king of leather bags, and the title seems deserved.

In fact, the name "Dulles briefcase" originated in postwar Japan. John Foster Dulles, who visited Japan as an American envoy concerned with the peace settlement, carried this style of metal-framed briefcase. Later, the second-generation proprietor of Ginza TANIZAWA, [name transliteration uncertain], conceived of a new version. After repeated attempts and failures, it went on sale in Showa 26 (1951) as the Dulles briefcase.

The name was taken by the Japanese from the name of the person associated with the model. In Europe and the United States, this type of metal-framed leather bag is known as a "doctor's bag" or "lawyer's bag."

Photograph captions:

- U.S. Secretary of State Dulles carrying a Dulles briefcase. Photograph courtesy of Ginza Tanizawa.
- Ginza TANIZAWA shop at the time of the product's launch. On the left is the originator of the Dulles briefcase, the second-generation proprietor. Photograph courtesy of Ginza Tanizawa.

PDF PAGE 16 | USING A DULLES BRIEFCASE
======================================================================
Heading: Using a Dulles Briefcase

I can no longer clearly remember when I first began using a Dulles briefcase, or when I realized that I could carry this bag around and become fascinated by it. Its form, appearance, and the way it conveys a gentleman's bearing may be part of the appeal. Perhaps, even without meaning to, carrying one makes a man feel a little more like a grown man.

Thinking about it carefully, I have only had it repaired once. I certainly have not handled it with excessive care, yet it has never complained. It has accompanied me wherever I have gone. When meeting someone, the briefcase can even take the place of a business card. As long as I have it with me, I can carry most of the things I need. In a phrase, it is "quite dependable."

Although I normally do not notice much, I suddenly find it has acquired more character with age. It seems to reflect my own growth, and brings a feeling of pleasure and satisfaction. Simply owning a Dulles briefcase may symbolize opening the door to adulthood and maturity.

======================================================================
Heading: The Process and Key Points of Making a Dulles Briefcase

This book follows the Dulles briefcase-making process of Mr. Kameda of the hand-stitched leather-goods workshop TRUNK. His methods are presented in a form that ordinary readers can understand, together with the information required to make the bag.

The book uses materials and tools common to leathercraft, although some operations require specialized equipment. It is intended to make it possible for readers to challenge themselves to make a Dulles briefcase. To show its exceptional appeal and beauty, however, the text approaches the construction from the perspective of a professional maker and explains the techniques and details.

Although the book supplies the information needed from beginning to completion, it does not simplify difficult operations or omit steps that are hard to explain. Readers who carefully observe Mr. Kameda's technique will discover additional details beyond the written instructions.

As the saying goes, even a journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step. If every step is handled carefully, the work can proceed toward completion.

The pages that follow first give an overview of the complete process, from starting work to finishing the bag. Use it to understand the general order and key points before arranging your own work plan.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 19 | PROCESS OUTLINE, I-III
======================================================================
I. Understanding the Parts and Their Structure

First, read the explanations of the structure of each component and grasp the various elements of the Dulles briefcase. With the relevant knowledge in hand, you can judge the steps you should practice, the operations that require assistance from others, and other production details for yourself.

II. Preparing Materials and Tools

The principal materials are leather, metal fittings, steel or aluminum frame stock, and core/reinforcement materials. The book explains the required types and quantities so that they can be prepared accordingly. In addition to ordinary leathercraft tools, certain special tools are required, some for only part of the process. Metalworking tools needed to fabricate the opening frame must also be prepared. Their uses and frequency of use are described later.

III. Organizing the Work Environment

Besides materials and tools, prepare a suitable working environment. A space roughly the size of an ordinary dining table is sufficient. Pay attention to noise during hammering, dust, and waste. Adequate ventilation and working at appropriate times will prevent most problems. Having a dedicated place where work can be resumed at any time greatly improves efficiency and convenience.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 20 | PROCESS OUTLINE, IV-VI
======================================================================
IV. Repeated Experimentation and Practice

Although not strictly necessary, readers are encouraged to make trial pieces first, to deepen their understanding of the construction process and of the Dulles briefcase itself. Practicing before beginning the main operations lets you experiment with techniques and avoid wasting time and money. Even professionals first make prototypes with scrap leather before making a new bag for the first time. Greater familiarity with the techniques produces a more complete finished work.

V. Making the Leather Components

Once production begins, first make the individual leather components. Apart from the common techniques of cutting, gluing, stitching and edge finishing, some operations involve shrinking or forming the leather and stretching it over three-dimensional objects. Because the Dulles briefcase is hand stitched throughout, about 90 percent of the work is completed during this stage.

VI. Installing the Lock

The lock is introduced as a separate step because fitting it to the front of the bag is one of the most distinctive parts of the construction. It is not particularly difficult, but the lock may be considered the "identity" of the Dulles briefcase and must be installed carefully. This book installs the lock using the traditional method of peening/setting nails.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 21 | PROCESS OUTLINE, VII-IX
======================================================================
VII. Making Metal Strips into the Opening Frame

To some extent, the opening frame is the most difficult component in this book. It requires cutting, drilling and bending metal, operations beyond ordinary leathercraft. Some metalworking tools are needed, so readers should study the tools carefully. However, frame making does not call for exceptional skills, its steps are relatively simple, and there is no need to worry excessively.

VIII. Assembly and Completion

Putting all the components together and giving the bag its final shape is the climax of making a leather bag. Check the overall balance while imagining the beautiful form you have worked toward. In fact, compared with making the individual parts, this operation is not especially difficult. Complete it with the satisfaction of fitting the last pieces of a jigsaw puzzle together.

IX. Use

Finishing the bag is not the final step. A Dulles briefcase realizes its true value only when it is picked up and used. Whether for yourself or as a gift, a Dulles briefcase ages and matures rather than simply deteriorating. A hand-stitched leather bag has the potential to grow richer with age. It is the maker's craftsmanship together with the user's care that truly completes it.

======================================================================
Heading: The First Step in Tackling a Dulles Briefcase Is to Understand Your Opponent

Before doing any actual work, first identify all the components of the Dulles briefcase. Knowing which materials are used, how much is required, and how everything is assembled will make the subsequent work much more efficient.

In this book, the required quantity of each part is identified. This gives readers a basis for preparing materials, although the figures are not scaled to exact proportions and should not be treated as such. When you are uncertain about how parts will be assembled or what shape should be cut, return to the photographs at the beginning of the book. Even when your opponent seems formidable, a clear understanding of it is a source of strength.

If you separate all the components of the Dulles briefcase, the number may seem unexpectedly large. Yet each small part will eventually be shaped and assembled into a solid leather bag. A skillfully constructed Dulles briefcase should satisfy your curiosity about how it works.

Photograph caption:
Once all the parts have been measured, they can be cut from a single piece of leather (half hide or shoulder leather). Consider the direction in which the leather will stretch and arrange the cut pieces to avoid wasting material. This is an important skill for a leatherworker.

The bag body consists of front and rear panels. Each is made by bonding three layers: face leather, core, and lining leather. Although the two panels look like they are the same size, take care to make the rear panel slightly larger than the front. In addition, the core and lining should be smaller than the exterior leather. The main lock fitting is mounted on the front panel, with an additional piece of leather adhered to the inside to reinforce the protruding fitting.

PDF PAGE 27 | HANDLE
======================================================================
Heading: Handle

Material labels:
- Central core material (face leather or core leather)
- Base / form leather (face leather or core leather)
- Layered padding/filler leather (face leather or core leather)
- Outer wrapping leather (face leather)
- Root/end wrapping leather (face leather)

The handle is among the most intricate and difficult components of the bag and involves many pieces. Once its construction is understood, however, the work becomes less intimidating.

The base/form leather is folded to make the handle body. Additional layers of padding leather are applied to both sides to build its thickness, while the central core is inserted into the middle. The two ends of the form leather, which fold around the root of the handle, are covered with end-wrapping leather. Once the components are assembled, the whole handle is sewn into its final outer leather covering.

Captions:
1. Place the formed padding leather on both sides of the handle's base/form leather.
2. Fold the base/form leather and sew the end-wrapping leather around the folded root of the handle.
3. Insert the central core into the middle of the base to create the handle's shape.
4. Sew on the full outer wrapping leather.

Heading: Preparing Materials

This section introduces the materials needed for the Dulles briefcase. Aside from metal fittings and the metal strips used for the opening frame, most of them are natural materials, especially leather. Some adhesives and sewing threads are also made from natural materials, which are worth examining closely.

Take particular care in choosing the leather. Its quality directly affects the appearance and character of the finished bag. Choose good-quality leather within your budget, but consider not just its appearance: also consider how difficult it will be to cut, shape and work with. Many types can be used if they meet the necessary requirements, but some are much easier to handle than others. Beginners should start with manageable leather. The materials described here are commercially available. Examine and feel the available choices to find materials suited to the work.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 32 | METAL FITTINGS AND FRAME STRIPS
======================================================================
Headings: Metal Fittings; Metal Strips (for the Opening Frame)

Three types of hardware are required: the lock, handle mounts and frame pivot fasteners. The lock is the type in which a projecting catch is inserted into the body of the fitting. It need not be exactly the same model shown, provided its dimensions and design are appropriate. Locks may be attached by rivets, screws or folded metal tabs. This example uses a riveted type.

The handle mounts attach to the opening frame and carry the handle. The example uses mounts approximately 20–21 mm wide. The frame pivots consist of a bolt and nut, connecting the two frame sections so they can open and close. A medium-sized type is used here. Match the metalwork in scale, style and colour to the leather. These items are available from leather-supply or hardware specialists.

Note: The fittings shown in the book were specially made for TRUNK and are not commercially available.

Metal strips for the frame are flat bar stock. The exterior frame carries the handles and therefore needs strength, so steel is specified. The inner frame can be made from lighter aluminium to reduce the bag's weight. [Further specifications on the lower part of the page are not sufficiently legible.]

Labels: key; rivets/pins; lock faceplate; lock body; handle mount; frame pivot.

PDF PAGE 33 | EXTERIOR LEATHER AND HANDLE-CORE LEATHER
======================================================================
Headings: Leather for Exposed Surfaces; Leather Thickness; Core Leather

For the leather visible on the outside of the bag, vegetable-tanned leather is recommended. Natural-finish leather, dyed leather and oil-finished leather are all possible, depending on the desired appearance. Firmer leather helps the briefcase hold its dignified, structured shape, but it becomes correspondingly harder to bend, wrap and work. Beginners will have an easier time with leather that retains some flexibility.

The book uses English bridle leather, which suits the Dulles silhouette especially well, though it is quite stiff and difficult to handle. Its finish may also resist stretching. In the patterns, this material is identified as "exterior leather" or "surface leather."

Pattern thicknesses are guide values, not immutable specifications. They reflect the maker's choice of look, feel and workability. Adjust for the actual hides and processes available. It may be difficult to achieve precise thicknesses without access to a splitting service, so choose suitable available weights and refer to the indicated thicknesses as a starting point.

For the handle's built-up core, choose leather with the required substance but preferably somewhat softer than the exterior leather. Thick split leather or other core-quality leather can be suitable. A thickness of around 3 mm is mentioned. Some handle pieces labelled "exterior leather" in the pattern refer to this core-building material rather than the visible finished surface.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 34 | LINING LEATHER AND REINFORCEMENT
======================================================================
Headings: Lining Leather; Core/Reinforcement Material

For the interior, a thin but durable, abrasion-resistant leather is suitable. The book recommends pigskin, which is economical, comparatively easy to work with and can give a refined finish. Select a colour that complements the exterior. In the patterns, "lining leather" is used on the exposed surfaces of the bottom board and partitions.

For the body panels, partitions and bottom board, a reinforcing sheet is used. The text names BON-TEX, approximately 1 mm thick, which can be purchased from leather suppliers and craft shops. Similar materials may also be tried. Changing the material's thickness or number of layers changes stiffness and feel. Avoid an excessively rigid material, such as inflexible ordinary cardboard, if it prevents the finished bag from flexing appropriately.

Heading: Preparing Tools

Not every tool shown must be bought immediately. Some can be replaced by tools already in your workshop; others can be omitted or substituted according to the job and your own working preferences. The following pages explain how frequently each tool is used, its purpose and possible alternatives so you can make informed choices.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 36 | BASIC TOOLS: CUTTING AND GLUING
======================================================================
Headings: Basic Tools (1); General Work and Cutting; Adhesion

Basic tools are the ones used repeatedly in leathercraft. Prepare these even if you do not acquire every specialist tool. Their functions will not be re-explained at every step later in the book.

A round awl is used to make pilot holes, marks and shallow lines. A diamond awl, whose point has a diamond-shaped cross-section, is used to open holes for hand stitching. A bone folder or pressure spatula helps shape leather and press glued surfaces. Other essentials include a ruler, leather knife or utility knife, and a glass plate for skiving and working leather. A leather knife may also be used for thinning leather; the utility knife and other scrapers can assist with roughing surfaces.

The principal adhesive is a strong rubber-based contact cement, rather than a temporary rubber glue. Because leather stretches and flexes, the adhesive must hold securely under movement. Woodworking PVA glue is useful for fixing thread and other small tasks. Apply adhesive with an appropriate spreader. After joining the glued surfaces, press them together firmly, for example with a roller, so the bond develops properly.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 37 | BASIC TOOLS: HAND STITCHING
======================================================================
Heading: Basic Tools (2): Hand Sewing

To sew along a leather edge, first use an edge creaser or adjustable marking gauge to scribe a line parallel to the edge. Stitching chisels or pricking irons are used to establish evenly spaced holes along this line. The diamond-shaped teeth are driven into the leather with a wooden mallet. The example discusses roughly 4 mm spacing; select a suitable iron and tooth count for the work.

A stitching pony or clamp holds the work so both hands remain free. Use blunt-tipped leather hand-sewing needles and a suitable thread. The book uses natural linen thread, though synthetic alternatives exist. Thread size changes the visible character of the stitches. The example uses three strands of No. 20 linen thread, roughly comparable in size to No. 7 thread. Wax thread to reduce abrasion, fraying, staining and tangling; pre-waxed thread is also available. Ordinary scissors will cut thread, but sharp thread snips leave cleaner ends.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 38 | BASIC TOOLS: EDGE FINISHING
======================================================================
Heading: Basic Tools (continued): Finishing Exposed Edges

The cut cross-section of leather is called the edge. In this project, many visible edges are coloured and burnished to a shine. Other approaches, such as turning or binding edges, can conceal the cut surface entirely.

Use a plane or edge-trimming tool to level a joint between two or more leather layers. Sand progressively to refine the surface. Approximately 150- and 400-grit abrasive papers are mentioned; a higher grit number is finer. Attaching abrasive paper to a block or triangular sponge helps keep the work controlled.

Choose edge dye that harmonizes with the exterior leather, often the same colour or slightly darker, and apply it neatly with a cotton swab. An edge-finishing agent suppresses loose fibres, strengthens the edge and adds lustre. A natural alternative is tragacanth gum. After applying it, rub with cloth or canvas to burnish. An optional heated edge creaser can both compact the edge and add decorative lines. It may be heated using an alcohol lamp. A final touch of wax can provide a protective sheen.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 39 | SPECIAL TOOLS
======================================================================
Heading: Special Tools

These are tools used only in particular stages, some essential and others replaceable. You can decide which ones to acquire as the need arises.

Pliers and nippers are used when fitting the lock, setting rivets and installing the frame pivots. Hollow punches make round holes in leather. PDF PAGE 54 | TAB
======================================================================
Heading: Tab

The next part is the small pull tab fixed at the centre of the front panel. Although it is partly concealed beneath the clasp, it is a characteristic feature of the Dulles briefcase. It is used to pull the mouth of the bag open and must therefore be stitched securely. Because the fingers regularly touch this part, its thickness and feel also deserve attention.

The principal methods are consistent, even step skiving around the perimeter, pressing the skived parts together thoroughly, careful bonding and stitching, and neat edge finishing. A useful technique is to cut one matching piece exactly to size and the second a little larger. After bonding, trim the larger piece flush with the smaller one. This extra material is referred to as an allowance or surplus edge. It accommodates slight misalignment and helps produce a clean finished contour. The term is also encountered in other crafts and trades.

Heading: Flap (Tongue Clasp)

The tongue-shaped flap holds the bag opening closed. Its construction resembles the pull tab: skive and glue two leather pieces, then stitch and finish them. It introduces an important additional technique, bonding while curved. The flap is used bent over the bag body. If the layers are bonded while completely flat and later forced to curve, the inner surface may wrinkle and the outside may pull tight, damaging both appearance and durability. Bonding the layers in the desired curvature builds that shape into the component.

The decorative metal lock plate also mounts to the flap. Depending on the fitting, it may be held by screws, bent tabs or rivets. The book uses a custom TRUNK plate secured with brass pins. Insert the pins, trim their projecting ends to roughly 1 mm and peen the exposed ends carefully. Avoid striking the decorative face or scratching the metal. A screw-mounted alternative is simpler to install, but its geometry may require modifying the leather pattern.

PDF PAGE 80 | HANDLE
======================================================================
Heading: Handle

The handle is one of the most interesting parts of making the Dulles bag. It is a smooth, three-dimensional shape created entirely from pieces of essentially flat leather. The maker exploits leather's ability to stretch, compress and conform, building a sculptural form with both strength and delicacy.

Although the result looks like specialized work, the process is a careful combination of familiar operations: cutting, bonding, skiving, shaping, sanding and sewing. Basic tools are sufficient, although calipers make it easier to check thickness and symmetry.

The important principle is balance. The built-up core must be symmetrical and have the correct volume before the outer covering is fitted. The covering leather must then be worked gradually around it, allowing the material to take its intended shape without overstretching or forcing a badly fitting piece.

Heading: Frame

The last major component is the rigid metal opening frame, the structural heart of the Dulles briefcase. It is made from steel and aluminium flat bars that are cut, drilled and bent, then covered with leather. This stage moves beyond ordinary leathercraft into metalworking.

PDF PAGE 164 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: CUTTING LEATHER
======================================================================
Heading: Basic Techniques; Using Tools for Accurate Cutting

This chapter revisits operations used repeatedly throughout the project and explains ways of performing them more consistently. It also shares the supervising artisan's rules of thumb. Even experienced makers may find ways to improve their results.

Every component begins with cutting. Transfer the pattern accurately to the hide with an awl, then cut along the marking line. Small cutting errors make subsequent bonding and assembly harder, especially where several pieces must align. A well-maintained leather knife or sharp utility knife is the foundation of precise work.

For straight cuts, use a ruler as a guide. Pull the leather knife smoothly toward yourself, accounting for the blade's cutting angle without forcing it. For very long lines, reposition the ruler in sections rather than attempting one uninterrupted cut. Cut thick leather in several controlled passes if needed. For curves, use a shorter controlled motion and a grip that allows fine adjustment. Keep the knife edge sharp; the quality of skiving in particular depends heavily on the condition of the blade.

Photo captions: using a leather knife along a ruler; cutting curves; correct utility-knife angle; repositioning the ruler for a long cut.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 165 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: APPLYING ADHESIVE
======================================================================
Heading: Gluing Leather with Rubber Contact Cement

Apply rubber contact cement with a spreader, moving it across the leather in a controlled, even layer. The angle of the spreader affects how much cement remains on the surface. The best condition is for the adhesive to penetrate the leather slightly while still leaving enough material at the surface to form a bond.

Contact cement grips as the surfaces touch, so misalignment can be difficult or impossible to correct. This is why paper-pattern separators and staged bonding are used throughout the book. Some jobs, such as positioning the flap at the bag opening or fixing thread ends, instead require woodworking white glue, which permits adjustment before it sets.

The consistency of cement also matters. Solvent evaporates while a container stands open, making it thicker. Thin adhesive spreads easily but may bond less strongly; thick adhesive holds firmly but is harder to apply evenly. Use the proper solvent to adjust viscosity when needed, and learn to vary spreading pressure to suit the condition of the glue.

Diagram: ideal glue application has some adhesive absorbed into the leather and some remaining on its surface.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 166 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: STITCH MARKS AND PRICKING IRONS
======================================================================
Headings: Drawing Lines with an Edge Gauge; Making Clean Stitch Holes

When sewing along a leather edge, mark a parallel stitching line with an adjustable edge gauge. One point rides against the cut edge while the other marks the line. The scribed line only needs to be visible; avoid cutting a deep groove into the surface.

Simply measuring the gap between the gauge's tips may not produce the intended distance in use. A more accurate method is to mark the desired offset from the edge with a ruler, adjust the gauge to that mark while its guide point rests against the actual leather edge, then draw the line.

The book uses a pricking iron primarily to make shallow diamond-shaped impressions, not to punch enormous holes all the way through thick leather. During hand sewing, open the holes with a diamond awl as necessary. Driving the iron deeply through thick leather distorts the surrounding surface and creates ugly oversized holes. Use a multi-tooth iron for straight lines, and a two-tooth iron for curves and corners. Strike with controlled, light blows from a mallet.

Photo captions: setting gauge from a ruler mark; straight and curved hole patterns; comparison of excessive deep punching with proper shallow pricking.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 167 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: SPACING HOLES AT CORNERS
======================================================================
Heading: Rules for Even Stitch-Hole Placement

A skilled worker may prick with an awl and sew in the same motion, but beginners benefit from establishing or opening all holes carefully first. Keep the awl at a consistent angle, even when the leather is curved or too large to see both sides simultaneously.

To continue a straight row, overlap a tooth of the pricking iron with an existing mark so the pitch remains regular. When approaching a corner or the end of a line, begin adjusting spacing several holes in advance so that a hole lands exactly at the endpoint. Small changes in the iron's position can distribute the error without creating one unusually large gap.

At a sharp corner, the corner hole can serve as the beginning of the next row. For a rounded body-panel corner, imagine a smooth curve inside the drawn angle and arrange approximately three holes symmetrically along it. This produces a rounded stitch line instead of a rigid square corner.

Diagrams: continuation of pricking pattern; adjusting the last holes to reach a corner; evenly arranged holes on rounded corners.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 168 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: THREAD PREPARATION AND SADDLE STITCH
======================================================================
Headings: Waxing Thread; Thread Length; Saddle Stitching

Wax the thread before hand sewing, rubbing it against sewing wax several times until it feels slightly tacky. Wax reduces friction and abrasion as the thread repeatedly moves through leather, and helps resist dirt and tangling. Pre-waxed thread can also be purchased.

A starting estimate for saddle-stitch thread length is a little more than four times the length of the seam, with extra allowance for unusually thick leather. Avoid extremely long lengths, which become dirty and abraded by repeated handling. The author discusses an older measure, one "fathom," approximately an adult's full arm span or 1.818 m. Long seams can be sewn in sections.

Saddle stitch uses a needle at each end of the thread, with both needles passed from opposite sides through successive holes. Open each hole with a diamond awl close to the time of sewing, since leather can close partially after being pricked. Use enough tension to seat the thread evenly but not so much that soft leather puckers or deforms.

At heavily stressed transitions, reinforce the seam by making an extra pass with one thread before continuing. When holes have been pricked from opposing sides of a thick piece, use the awl carefully to avoid unwanted cross-shaped holes at the exit face.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 169 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: FINISHING STITCHES
======================================================================
Heading: Backstitching and Securing Thread Ends

The end of a seam is more prone to loosening than its middle. Reinforce the last several stitches with a backstitch, generally working back about three holes. One thread is passed backward through the existing holes while the other thread is temporarily left loose. Form a small loop and pass the needle through it inside the stitch hole to create a concealed locking knot.

Apply a little woodworking white glue to the thread within the hole, then pull the thread ends tight and trim them very close to the leather. Tuck any exposed fibres down into the hole with an awl to obtain a tidy finish. The photos show the thread sequence across nine small steps.

Photo sequence: stitch to the stopping point; reverse through several holes; make concealed loop; apply white glue; tension, cut and bury the ends.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 170 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: LEVELLING CUT EDGES
======================================================================
Heading: Why Edges Are Finished; Planing and Sanding

Finishing an edge improves not only its appearance but also its durability. Bags are subjected to impacts, bending and pressure, which can separate the layers of a laminated cut edge. Rounding, compressing and burnishing the edge helps the layers resist damage.

Before polishing, level any stepped joint or protruding leather so the bonded layers form a continuous surface. If an uneven edge is left as it is, dye and polish will make the irregularity more visible rather than conceal it. An edge plane can remove material quickly and help establish a neat profile; set its blade to take very shallow cuts and use steady, controlled strokes. Adjust the blade exposure carefully, rather than removing too much leather at once.

The edge plane is also useful for shaping handle padding, but finish with abrasives to avoid leaving planing marks.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 171 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: DYE AND BURNISHING
======================================================================
Heading: Colouring and Strengthening Leather Edges

Choose an edge dye that suits the leather and the intended appearance. Apply only as much as the cotton swab or applicator can hold without dripping. Excess dye may run onto the grain face or stain light-coloured thread. Work along the full edge and let the colour penetrate evenly.

Next use tragacanth or another edge-finishing product to compact the loose fibres. The author favours a natural agent rather than a heavy acrylic or polyurethane coating. Rub vigorously with cloth or canvas while the treatment remains workable. Friction and pressure smooth the fine irregularities and produce a firmer, more lustrous edge.

Place the work on a cork block to keep it stable, especially when pressing sideways. A hard bone folder can also work well on thick edges. Several cycles of fine sanding, edge treatment and burnishing produce a stronger, smoother finish than one quick pass. Stop when the result has the shape, texture and shine you want.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 172 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: HEATED CREASING AND WAX
======================================================================
Headings: Using a Decorative Edge Creaser; Final Wax Finish

A heated decorative edge creaser can both add a fine line and compact or harden the fibres close to the edge. Heat its tip over an alcohol lamp, then draw the appropriate groove along the leather edge like a small iron. The temperature needs care: too cool and it has little effect; too hot and it scorches or darkens the leather excessively. Test on scrap leather, especially when working with pale colours.

For lines on both faces of a part, consider the order of operations because applying heat to the second face may soften the first impression. Many artisans reshape the grooves on their own creasing tools with fine files to suit a preferred rounded or angular edge profile.

The final stage is to apply wax. The book distinguishes a refined, pale beeswax used on edges from the stickier wax commonly used on sewing thread. Apply a modest amount, warm it gently with the creaser so it melts into the leather, then polish with a clean cloth. Too much wax can leave blotches on the grain surface; the goal is a supple, lightly protected, glossy edge.

======================================================================
PDF PAGE 173 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: SETTING METAL FASTENERS
======================================================================
Heading: Installing Riveted Locks and Fittings

When fitting a lock or decorative faceplate, first transfer the fastening-hole positions precisely to the leather and make the required openings. Insert the hardware and support its visible metal face on a setting anvil protected by leather, so hammering does not scratch or dent it.

Trim the projecting pins with cutters, leaving just enough metal to form a head. Peen the ends a little at a time with a suitable metalworking hammer until the hardware is securely retained. Work one pin at a time where possible to stop the backplate from falling away. Keep loose metal fragments controlled, and avoid striking the decorative metal surface. The final rivet should be neatly flattened without sharp edges or conspicuous projections.

PDF PAGE 174 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: PARTIAL SKIVING
======================================================================
Heading: Skiving; Why Partial Skiving Matters

Skiving does not always mean reducing an entire hide to an even thickness. Much of bag making uses local, partial skiving around the edges of components. This allows several layers to join without creating a thick, awkward seam and produces a refined profile with controlled fullness in the middle.

Careful control of edge thickness is one of the principal skills of a bag maker. The book demonstrates methods that can be carried out with a sharp leather knife rather than requiring a leather splitting machine. Blade sharpness matters enormously: a dull blade pulls at the leather, wandering or tearing the intended cut.

Three skiving shapes are introduced. A step skive cuts down at a marked starting line and continues at roughly the target remaining thickness. A tapered skive progressively reduces thickness toward the cut edge, potentially to zero. A combined method uses both effects to control the appearance and shape of a multi-layer seam. The drawings show representative cross-sections.

PDF PAGE 175 | BASIC TECHNIQUES: STEP SKIVING AND PRESSING
======================================================================
Heading: Making the Step Skive and Using It Well

For a step skive, begin at the marked inner boundary, bring the blade down to the desired remaining thickness, then remove the outer strip with a controlled, nearly level cut. Work carefully so the bottom of the skive is even and the transition does not dig into the visible surface. Practice can eventually make the motion quick and continuous.

After bonding two step-skived layers, a hollow may remain beneath the edge. Press the joint firmly against a hard backing or thick paper guide with the corner of the bone folder. Eliminating the hollow creates a full, gently raised centre and an elegant thin perimeter. The diagrams show why pressing the space below the step is as important as the skiving itself.

Heading: The Unique Qualities and Appeal of Natural Leather

The editors ask TRUNK's craftsman, Yoshihiro Kameda [name transliteration approximate], what matters most when making a handsome, substantial Dulles briefcase.

Editor: What type of leather is suitable for a Dulles briefcase?

Kameda: Fairly firm, stiff leather is best. It helps the finished bag retain its form instead of collapsing.

Editor: The choice of leather must make a considerable difference. What draws you personally to leather?

Kameda: Its appeal lies in its being a natural material. Every hide has its own character. The tanning, finishing and techniques used by different makers bring out different qualities. Once it is made into a bag, the shape and colour continue to change with time. Like raw wood, leather can be worked into many forms. It is also flexible and bendable, which may give it even more possibilities than wood. The same raw material can present very different faces depending on how it is handled.

Editor: How long does it usually take to make the bag described in this book?

Kameda: Because I also teach classes and fulfil custom orders, I rarely spend an entire day on one bag, and I haven't precisely timed the work. If I advance the project every day, perhaps around two weeks.

Editor: Even a professional needs two weeks?

Kameda: Among leather bags, a Dulles is quite a substantial undertaking. The hardest thing is often to keep your motivation and determination until it is finished. Work steadily without becoming discouraged.

Heading: Choosing the Right Lock for a Dulles Briefcase

Editor: What do you look for in a lock?

Kameda: Demand for old-fashioned, traditional bag locks has declined, and manufacturers have reduced the number of types they produce. It is increasingly difficult to find one that I like. If you find an appealing old model, it may be wise to obtain it while it is still available.

Heading: Imagine the Finished Bag and Enjoy Making It

Editor: What led you to become a bag maker?

Kameda: My grandfather was a men's tailor, and his work influenced me. I was interested in fashion and design and wanted an occupation that used detailed work with my hands. I considered both footwear and bags, then chose bagmaking.

Editor: What surprised you when you began working in leather?

Kameda: The number of people from across the country asking to attend classes. I had not realized so many people were passionate about making their own bags.

Editor: What gives you the most satisfaction?

Kameda: Knowing that the person using the bag is happy. While you work, imagine how it will look when it is complete and how pleased you or the person receiving it as a gift will feel. That image will help you find the energy to finish it.

Title: The Book of Dulles Bag Making

Heading: What Is a Dulles Bag?

The name 'Dulles bag' is associated in Japan with the American statesman John Foster Dulles, who was known for carrying a distinctive, substantial briefcase. Similar wide-opening cases were historically used by doctors and other professionals. The Japanese term came to denote a formal leather briefcase with a rigid metal-framed mouth, a wide opening, a structured body, substantial handle and recognizable central closure.

The bag's appeal lies in its mixture of formality and practicality. The frame makes it easy to open the case and examine what is inside, while the leather, hardware and lock give the object a dignified appearance. This book documents the materials, techniques and careful handwork required to make such a bag.

Illustrations: Historical photograph, an outline diagram of the Dulles case and publisher/barcode information.
